Last updated: September 2026

This repository tracks notable commercial Warehouse Control Systems (WCS), hosted platforms, and open-source projects for warehouse automation and intralogistics.

Warehouse Control Systems sit between enterprise applications such as WMS, ERP, OMS, and MES and physical automation systems such as PLCs, conveyors, sorters, AS/RS, shuttle systems, AMRs, AGVs, robotic picking systems, AutoStore grids, and goods-to-person stations.

Examples include Swisslog SynQ, Dematic iQ, Körber WCS, Made4net WCS, Mecalux Easy WMS, WITRON WCS, Daifuku WCS, Fortna WCS, SSI SCHAEFER WAMAS, and KNAPP KiSoft (the category leaders).

Open-source emphasis: Commercial WCS software remains dominated by proprietary automation vendors, but the open-source ecosystem is growing. This list therefore includes not only dedicated open-source WCS projects, but also open WMS/MFC systems, industrial automation platforms, robotics middleware, AMR fleet-management frameworks, workflow engines, digital-twin tools, MQTT/OPC UA infrastructure, and warehouse software frameworks that can be combined to build vendor-neutral WCS solutions.

Additional Strong Open-Source Options

Warehouse and inventory systems: OpenWMS.org, OpenBoxes, ERPNext, Odoo Community Edition, OCA WMS modules, and community-developed inventory-management systems.

Dedicated warehouse control and material flow: openWCS, OpenWMS.org Material Flow Control, custom Spring Boot or Python microservices, and event-driven warehouse orchestration architectures.

Industrial connectivity: Apache PLC4X, Eclipse Milo, open62541, Node-RED, MQTT, OPC UA, and Modbus integration tools.

Robotics and AMRs: ROS 2, Open-RMF, Nav2, MoveIt, Gazebo, Webots, and community AMR frameworks.

IoT and edge computing: ThingsBoard, FIWARE, Eclipse Ditto, Mosquitto, EMQX, and edge-device platforms.

Event-driven architectures: Apache Kafka, RabbitMQ, MQTT, Redis Streams, and other message-driven architectures.

Workflow orchestration: Flowable, Camunda, Temporal, Apache Airflow, and BPMN-based process engines.

Digital twins: Eclipse Ditto, FIWARE Digital Twins, Gazebo simulations, Webots simulations, and custom Three.js warehouse visualizations.

Monitoring and analytics: Grafana, Prometheus, InfluxDB, OpenSearch, Apache Superset, and Metabase.

Computer vision for warehouses: OpenCV, YOLO implementations, TensorFlow, PyTorch, and ROS-based machine-vision pipelines.

Optimization and routing: Google OR-Tools, OptaPlanner, NetworkX, and custom operations-research models for task allocation and routing.

Custom automation APIs: FastAPI, Spring Boot, Django, Node.js, gRPC, GraphQL, and REST-based equipment adapters.

Frameworks for building custom systems: A strong vendor-neutral open-source WCS stack can combine OpenWMS.org or openWCS as the warehouse-control foundation, ERPNext or Odoo/OCA WMS as the ERP/WMS layer, Apache PLC4X or OPC UA for industrial connectivity, ROS 2 and Open-RMF for robots and AMRs, Kafka or RabbitMQ for real-time events, and Grafana + Prometheus + InfluxDB for operational visibility.

For highly automated facilities, a modular architecture is generally preferable: keep the WMS, WCS, equipment adapters, robot fleet manager, and analytics platform loosely coupled through APIs and event streams rather than tying the entire warehouse to one proprietary automation vendor.

Disclaimer

This is a community-curated list — not exhaustive and not an endorsement.

Warehouse Control Systems operate in environments involving conveyors, robotics, autonomous vehicles, PLCs, AS/RS systems, and other industrial equipment; software failures can create significant operational and safety risks.

Open-source WMS software is not automatically a full replacement for an enterprise WCS. Real-time equipment orchestration, deterministic control requirements, safety integration, PLC protocols, and vendor-specific automation often require additional engineering.

Self-hosted open-source systems require appropriate cybersecurity, industrial network segmentation, access control, monitoring, testing, redundancy, and disaster-recovery planning.

Critical safety functions should remain appropriately engineered and certified within industrial control and functional-safety architectures.
